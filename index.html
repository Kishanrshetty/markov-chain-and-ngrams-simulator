Markov chains · 1913

Does a letter remember the one before it?

★

In January 1913 Andrey Markov took the first 20,000 letters of Pushkin's Eugene Onegin, crossed out the spaces, and sorted every letter into two piles: vowel or consonant. Then he counted the pairs, all by hand. The reader below does the same thing in a few seconds.

Did you know?

Markov had no machine. He wrote the letters out by hand, ruled them into two hundred grids of ten by ten, and counted.

He was 56, and out to win an argument.

Why this mattered

Before Markov, the mathematics of chance mostly assumed every trial was independent: a coin does not remember its last flip. Markov showed you can still do serious mathematics when the trials do remember. A letter leans on the one before it, and that lean is measurable, stable, and different for every language.

That idea (state, transition, probability) is the ancestor of weather models, Google's original PageRank, speech recognition, and the autocomplete on your phone. It started with a man counting a poem by hand.

What this page is doing, exactly

Russian. Chapter 1 and the first 16 stanzas of Chapter 2 of Eugene Onegin, the passage Markov himself used, from Russian Wikisource: 19,893 letters. Following Markov, the soft and hard signs (ь, ъ) are dropped, and й is counted as a vowel, since it is и краткое, "short i". That choice matters: count й as a consonant and P(V→V) falls to 0.075 instead of Markov's 0.128.

English. The opening of Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (Project Gutenberg), 20,042 letters, with a, e, i, o and u as vowels and y as a consonant. The English reference row was measured for this page across Alice, The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes and Peter Pan together: 734,039 letters.

Both. Spaces, punctuation and digits are skipped, so the last letter of one word sits next to the first letter of the next, exactly as in Markov's stripped string.

n-grams · 1948

Shannon's ladder of fake English

★

In 1948 Claude Shannon asked a strange question: how much of English could you fake if all you had were counts? He imagined a series of machines, each allowed to remember a little more than the last, and worked out by hand what each would babble. As you climb, nonsense slowly grows a spine. Every rung below is generated fresh, right now, from about 160,000 characters of real books.

Did you know?

Shannon had no computer to do this. He opened a book at a random page, picked a letter, turned to another page, read until that letter turned up, and wrote down what followed. Again and again.

He stopped at letter triples because, as he put it, the labour was becoming enormous.

The ladder never quite reaches the top

Rung 6 sounds startlingly like a person until you try to follow the argument. It has learned which words like to sit next to each other, and nothing at all about what any of them mean. That gap (fluent, confident, and not actually about anything) is the oldest known problem in this field, and Shannon spotted it in the same paper that invented the subject.

Modern language models are on a much higher rung of the same ladder. They look back over thousands of symbols instead of three, and they learn the weights rather than counting them. But the shape of the trick is the one on this page.

What this page is doing, exactly

The tables are built in your browser when you press Generate, from 160,000 characters (31,686 words) of Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, Peter Pan and The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes (Project Gutenberg), reduced to 26 letters and the space: the same 27-symbol alphabet Shannon used. Rung 0 draws all 27 symbols equally. Rungs 1–4 draw each letter from the counts that follow the last 0, 1, 2 or 3 letters. Rung 5 draws whole words by how common they are; rung 6 draws each word from the counts that follow the word before it.

Shannon built his samples by hand, and his 1948 paper climbs rungs 0–3, then 5 and 6. Rung 4 is a bonus step he never took: four-letter tables were exactly where the labour became too much. His samples broke into recognisable words at about the same place as these do.

Build your own. Your sentences are split into words (capital letters ignored) and into sentences at full stops, question marks, exclamation marks and line breaks. The machine counts which word follows which, including which words start a sentence and which end one, and draws every next word from those counts: rung 6's rule, with your sentences as the whole library.
